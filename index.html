Alcester, SD 2026 Housing Study

📄 Print/PDF Version

Executive Summary

Alcester is a quiet, safe small town close enough to Sioux Falls and Sioux City for a quick trip or daily commute, but far enough away to have a real small-town feel. With a beautiful new school building and a different pace of life than the big cities, there are more people trying to move to Alcester than there are houses available.

As Alcester plans for the future, PorchLight was contracted to assess the upcoming housing needs of the community. To conduct this assessment, PorchLight analyzed existing data, conducted a visual inspection of housing structures in town, and collected input through interviews. This report analyzes the period from 2024-2034.

Based on our research and analysis, we recommend the following for Alcester:

1. Build 16 townhomes or duplexes prioritizing affordability and senior access.

2. Establish lots for 18 detached single-family homes.

3. Secure tax-credit funding for four affordable rental units for low-income households, either as a quad-plex or as part of a larger apartment building.

4. Build 14 rental units priced at $800-$1,200 per month.

5. Build 14 rental units priced at $1,200+ per month.

6. Approach housing needs cautiously, but comprehensively.

7. Proactively address rental management in the community.

Key Takeaways:

  • Population and Household Trends: We expect Alcester’s growth rate from 2024-2034 to be 0.65% per year, growing by approximately 6-7 people per year, assuming that necessary housing to support that population growth exists. To support that growth, we expect Alcester to require 3-4 new housing units per year.

  • Latent Demand: Based on interviews in the community, we see a high likelihood demand for housing in Alcester has been suppressed by a lack of available housing. Households have struggled to secure rental housing and land to build new owner-occupied homes, related only to availability and not price. This inability to secure housing indicates that more people would live in Alcester if they could. However, such demand may take some time to materialize after new housing has been built while the market resets to a new reality where housing is available in Alcester.

Alcester has low vacancy rates across the board. We typically recommend a 2% vacancy rate for owner-occupied housing, but Alcester’s rate is less than half of that at 0.65%. We also recommend a 5-8% vacancy rate for rental units, but Alcester’s rental unit vacancy is somewhere between 0 and 2%. This means the housing market has no slack to absorb new arrivals.

  • Key Demographics: Two key demographics that require additional housing in Alcester are first-time home buyers and seniors looking to downsize. In both cases, there is little to no available housing for them to make a move.

Why 2024?

Of note, while this report was written in 2026, the modeling in this report covers the years 2024-2034. This is because the best and most granular source of housing data for rural communities, the American Community Survey, is not released in real time. At the time of the writing of this report, the most recent data available was for 2024, so we look at a ten-year period of 2024 to 2034. Some of the data in this report is more recent, including information from 2025, but the core modeling of the report is based on 2024 data, and we choose to project out 10 years from that data.

Read on through the rest of the report for our full recommendations, a detailed housing needs analysis, and an appendix full of data and additional analysis.

Recommendations

Based on the analysis contained in this report, we recommend the following for the next ten years in Alcester:

1. Build 16 townhomes or duplexes prioritizing affordability and senior access.

Townhomes and duplexes provide more affordable pathways to building owner-occupied homes. For Alcester, townhomes and duplexes can provide a solution for two key housing challenges in the community: housing for seniors and entry-level homeownership.

Attached single-family homes (townhomes or duplexes) can help to serve households with incomes below $100,000 seeking to purchase a home in the community, likely for below $330,000. For a first-time home buyer in Alcester, a townhome can allow them to purchase a home and start building equity at a lower cost than a detached single-family home. Additionally, townhomes or duplexes can provide an attractive option for seniors in the community who are looking to downsize. This in turn can open up prime detached single-family housing in the community for young families.

Based on our modeling, we see demand for about 16 units in this price range.

2. Establish lots for 18 detached single-family homes.

For households with incomes above $100,000, we see demand for about 18 owner-occupied units. These households will most likely be looking for detached single-family homes priced above $300,000, with many looking for homes priced at $400,000 and above. This market will be best served with lot availability for custom-built homes. We anticipate about 11 of these households to have incomes of $100,000-$150,000 and about seven (7) to have incomes above $150,000.

3. Secure tax-credit funding for four affordable rental units for low-income households, either as a quad-plex or as part of a larger apartment building. Alcester has a reasonable amount of rental housing affordable for low-income families. We see a need for 4 more units affordable to households with incomes of $15,000-$35,000, renting for $250-$875 per month.

This could be achieved through a tax-credit application either for a standalone quad-plex or to integrate low-income units into a larger rental project.

4. Build 14 rental units priced at $800-$1,200 per month. For households with incomes of $50,000-$75,000, we see a need for an additional 14 rental units in Alcester, likely renting for $800-$1,200 per month. Right now, employers and community members report there is a lack of availability of rental units in the community. Rental units provide essential liquidity in the market, allowing new arrivals - for example, a new teacher in the community - to quickly get housing in the community while they establish roots, get settled, and evaluate the market for purchasing a home.

Typically, 5-8% vacancy among rentals is considered healthy for a community, indicating that the average rental unit is vacant for one month every 12-18 months. This ensures there are some openings for new arrivals without the financial strain for property owners of lots of vacant units.

5. Build 14 rental units priced at $1,200+ per month. Alcester also has a need for rental housing to serve the needs of households with incomes above $75,000. We see a need for 14 rental units at this price range.

These households are likely looking for larger units at a higher price point, upwards of $1,200. These units could include detached single-family homes or duplexes or townhomes available for rent, or it could include nicer, larger apartments in an apartment building.

6. Approach housing needs cautiously, but comprehensively. Alcester has housing needs across a variety of price points and housing types, from apartments and townhomes to premium detached single-family homes. Based on existing data and demographic trends, we can see Alcester supporting a new housing development with 32 apartment units, 16 townhome or duplex units, and 18 detached single-family homes over the course of a ten-year period. This works out to about three and a half attached or detached single-family homes per year and a little over three new apartments per year. This would be an increase from Alcester’s current new housing construction rate by about one single-family unit per year and a more significant increase in the number of apartments being built to address the more significant shortage in rental housing.

Alcester has had real restrictions on its growth over the past few years with limited lot availability and a low rental vacancy rate. It took the community a while to fill its previous housing development, but our input interviews also identified little marketing outside the community of those lots. With more aggressive efforts to market and fill lots, Alcester likely has the underlying demand to fill a development over time.

7. Proactively address rental management in the community. One challenge community leaders face in Alcester is that many of the apartments in town are managed by a non-profit and maintained by volunteers. This is a significant benefit to the community but also risks burning out that core group of volunteers. We recommend that Alcester conduct long-term planning around stewardship of existing rental properties and consider options to attract high-quality property management companies to the community to share the load.

For households making less than $15,000 per year, our model projects a need for 21 owner-occupied units for those households to have housing that is affordable (20-30% of gross income) for them. Our model is identifying 36 owner-occupied households making less than $15,000, but only 16 owner-occupied homes that are affordable at that income. However, 78% of households making less than $15,000 in Alcester are aged 65 and older, so these are not households that will be taking on 30-year mortgages. In fact, these households likely hold a significant amount of their net worth in their owner-occupied housing. They do not necessarily have that housing fully paid off, but meeting the needs of this income bracket is likely best served through either owner-occupied or rental senior-friendly, less expensive units like apartments or townhomes to allow them to reach affordability.

For households making $15,000-$49,999, our model sees more housing units affordable than there are households in this range, with 92 units but only 49 households. Some of those units are occupied by households making less than $15,000, and some are occupied by households making more than $50,000. This portion of the owner-occupied unit mix lines up with reflections from community members that there are a significant number of smaller, older homes in Alcester. At current interest rates, these are homes valued at up to $165,000, so with current construction costs there is little to no risk that new builds will further saturate this market.

For households making $50,000-$74,999 we project a net need for 18 units. At today’s rates, these units could range from $110,000 to $246,000. A very efficient Governor’s Home project may be able to build a single-family home at this price, but more likely townhomes or manufactured homes will be required to achieve this price point.

For households making $75,000-$99,999 we project a net need of 20 units. At today’s rates, these units could range from $164,000 to $328,000. It may be possible to build single-family units within this range, but certainly townhomes or duplexes can accomplish this.

For households making $100,000-$149,000 we projected a net need of 11 units. At today’s rates, these units could range from $219,000 to $492,700. At this income level, we begin to reach the point where single-family homes can be custom-built, if the land is available.

For households making $150,000 and more we project a net need of 7 units. We expect these households would be looking for units at a price of $330,000 or greater, and likely quite a bit higher. The greatest challenge to meet this market need is land availability. 

We consider a unit to be a single-family home if it does not share vertical space with another structure or housing unit. This category includes traditional detached single-family homes that exist separately from any other housing units, but it also includes attached single-family homes like a duplex or townhome. (ACS Technical Documentation)

Multi-family units “are units in structures containing 2 or more housing units” where units share vertical space with other units. For example, this category could include a large or small apartment building, a four-plex with upstairs and downstairs units, or an apartment above a business. (ACS Technical Documentation)

Mobile homes are a nuanced piece of this housing mix analysis. Manufactured or mobile homes can provide a quick route to generating affordable housing, but can present challenges for a community around housing upkeep, utilities management, and storm resilience. For individuals, mobile homes can present as an opportunity to build home equity while in fact being a rapidly depreciating asset with potentially unaffordable maintenance and upkeep costs.

However, improvements in the quality of factory-built or prefabricated housing, especially when permanently installed on a more traditional lot, may present real affordable housing opportunities with limited downsides.

Initiatives like the Governor’s House program and modular housing help to achieve the affordability benefits of pre-fabricated homes with good quality materials and appropriate foundations and lots to serve as long-term, quality housing.

Implications for LIHTC Development

Under income averaging, a LIHTC project may designate individual units at income tiers ranging from 20% to 80% Area Median Income (AMI), provided the average income restriction across all tax credit units does not exceed 60% AMI. This approach allows developers to serve a broader income mix within a single project, which can strengthen underwriting in markets like Alcester where the rent differential between AMI tiers is relatively narrow.

Tax Credit Unit Recommendation

Our projections show a net need for 3-4 units affordable to households with incomes below $35,000. These households likely all fall below $34,300, which is the threshold for a 1-person household to be considered “Very Low Income” due to being at or below 40% of the Sioux City MSA’s Median Family Income.

As such, we believe that if developers can make a low-income quadplex pencil, it would be a competitive applicant for the Low Income Housing Tax Credit program.

F. Economic Analysis

i. Inflow/Outflow Analysis

Using commute data from the United States Census, PorchLight analyzed the commute patterns of Alcester workers and residents.

In total, there are 353 workers who live outside of Alcester but commute into the community for work. The US Census classifies these as “inflow” workers, and they comprise 92% of people who are employed in Alcester.

The other 32 people (8%) who are employed in Alcester live and work in Alcester. The US Census classifies these as “internal” workers.

Additionally, there are 323 people who live in Alcester but leave the community for work, meaning that 91% of workers who live in Alcester are employed outside the community. The US Census classifies these as “outflow” workers.

Net, 30 more people commute into Alcester for work than commute out of the community for work.

The biggest draw industries in Alcester are manufacturing and education.

The industries where Alcester sees the largest net outflow of workers are retail trade and accommodation and food services.

The top locations where Alcester workers live are Beresford (83), Sioux Falls (35), and Alcester (32).

The top locations where Alcester residents work are Sioux Falls (91), Beresford (69), and Alcester (32).

An important note: employment in a location is not the same as a daily commute to that location. Someone may, for example, be employed at a Sioux Falls organization but work from home in Alcester every day.

G. Vacancy Rates

According to the American Community Survey, in 2024, 28 housing units in Alcester were vacant. 2 were available for rent, 0 were for sale, and 26 were classified as “other vacant.”

This indicates an owner-occupied vacancy rate of 0% and a rental vacancy rate of 1.71%. In PorchLight’s visual inspection, 2 of 310 units inspected were for sale, which equates to a vacancy rate of 0.65%. Additionally, PorchLight’s conversations in the community confirmed that Alcester’s rental vacancy rate was very low, perhaps approaching zero.

Why Target 2% Owner-Occupied Vacancy?

A 2% vacancy rate for owner-occupied housing demonstrates some availability in the market without a significant number of vacant properties. The typical American homeowner spends about 13 years in their home, and up to 90 days on the market is considered an appropriate amount of time for a home to sell. Taking these factors into account, a home following a typical homeownership cycle will spend about 2% of time vacant. This indicates appropriate housing availability without long-term vacant housing.

Why Target 5-8% Rental Vacancy?
For renter-occupied units, we recommend a target vacancy rate of 5-8%. Rental units experience higher turnover and provide necessary slack in the housing market. A 5-8% vacancy rate indicates the average rental unit spends one month vacant every 12-20 months.

J. Community Input Summary

PorchLight conducted input interviews with a variety of individuals to develop a qualitative understanding of the housing market in Alcester alongside existing data. The following is a summary of those conversations, anonymized to allow individuals to speak freely. These conversations represent a variety of perspectives and do not necessarily reflect the views of the report authors.

i. Owner-Occupied Housing Market

Interviewees across the board identified that Alcester does not have enough owner-occupied housing right now. Standard housing that becomes available typically sells quickly, often in a pre-market sale, to an out-of-town buyer, or to a landlord who will convert it to a rental. Some houses in town have been for sale for a more significant period of time, but this seems to be more specific to those homes than to Alcester’s housing market.

Building entry-level owner-occupied housing in Alcester is a challenge due to high construction costs, which is not unique to Alcester. With that said, volunteers in Alcester have been working hard to bring new Governor’s Homes to the community and successfully getting some built, which introduces some entry-level housing.

Participants identified many of the homes on the west side of town as smaller, older, and less likely to be attractive housing for growing families.

ii. Renter-Occupied Housing Market

The rental market in Alcester was identified by participants across the board as lacking vacancy. From seniors who are on waitlists to move into rentals to new school staff living in basements, finding rental housing in Alcester is a challenge.

This is true even as the rental housing supply in Alcester expanded in recent years, as single-family homes on the west side of town converted from owner-occupied to rentals. However, many of these rentals are older homes and some community members have concerns about upkeep for those rentals.

Additionally, many of the rentals in the community are managed and kept up by volunteers who risk burning out.

One other challenge identified in the Alcester rental housing market is lack of involvement from larger property management companies. There appears to be room for more conversations with property management companies about building or owning rentals in Alcester, but so far there has been little interest.

iii. Attraction Factors

There are a few key factors participants identified for why people are interested in living in Alcester.

First is familiarity. Many folks who are looking to move to Alcester are originally from Alcester or have a family connection. These individuals are often looking for a familiar small-town lifestyle and a small-school environment for their children.

Second is location and proximity. Alcester is close enough to Sioux Falls or Sioux City for a commute, or serves as a good mid-point for a young couple splitting the difference between work in Sioux Falls and school in Vermillion.

Third is lifestyle. Alcester does have folks moving in from Sioux Falls and other larger communities nearby, and many of them are looking for small-town life, even as Alcester has familiar amenities like a grocery store, a school, and a pool.

iv. Senior Housing

Senior housing was identified as a win-win scenario for the community if it can be properly addressed. Participants confirmed they believe there are a good amount of seniors who would choose to downsize if there were availability. Duplexes or something similar with managed maintenance would likely move quickly in Alcester.

At the same time, this would help to open up some housing stock in Alcester that would be suitable for families.

v. Challenges for New Arrivers

Multiple families seeking to move to town identified severe challenges securing housing of any kind. Families we spoke to were eventually able to secure owner-occupied housing (or are on track to do so) by building their own homes, but even securing land was difficult, depending on family connections or unique circumstances.

The families we spoke to considered existing housing in Alcester, including over years-long periods, but were unable to find suitable housing for a family. This lack of availability extended to rental units.

Right now, in Alcester, buying a home essentially depends on getting fortunate with availability and building a home requires intense determination and creative problem-solving to secure land. With that said, once land is secured, we heard no complaints about the permitting process or securing builders for a new build in Alcester.

vi. Views on Potential Housing Development

There are mixed views on a potential housing development on the north side of town. Overall, participants agree the town needs more housing. The primary concern is about scale and cost - can the infrastructure be built out while still keeping lot costs low enough to move? Some would like to see a smaller development than the proposed option.

In general, there is a sense that lots alongside the golf course are highly desirable, but there is an upper limit to what people pay for them.

Others view the development as crucial for the growth of the town. In particular, some point to the fact that Alcester’s previous development filled, albeit slowly, without any traditional promotion. With proper promotion, some believe a new development would be successful at attracting new residents.

K. Methodology Notes

i. Population Modeling

To analyze historical population change and project future population trends, this study employs an age-cohort analysis using data from the American Community Survey (ACS). Population estimates are organized into ten-year age cohorts, structured to align with life-stage transitions and long-term demographic movement.

Age cohorts are defined as follows: ages 0–4, 5–14, 15–24, 25–34, 35–44, and continuing in ten-year increments through age 85 and older. This structure allows for consistent tracking of population cohorts over time while accounting for births, aging, migration, and mortality.

For each community, population counts are extracted for the most recent ACS year available (currently 2023) and for the same geography ten years prior (2013). To measure cohort change, each age group in the earlier year is compared to the corresponding group ten years older in the later year. For example, the population aged 25–34 in 2013 is compared to the population aged 35–44 in 2023. This comparison approximates the same cohort aging forward through time, allowing the analysis to capture net population change attributable to in-migration, out-migration, and survival effects.

Percentage changes are calculated for each cohort over the ten-year period. These cohort-specific change rates are then applied forward to project population by age ten years into the future. In practice, this means that observed historical trends—such as gains or losses as residents move from early adulthood into mid-career years—are applied to today’s younger cohorts to estimate how those populations are likely to evolve over the next decade.

Special adjustments are made for the youngest and oldest age groups. For the 0–4 age cohort, projections are based on the historical relationship between total population and the share of residents represented by early childhood ages, reflecting expected birth rates rather than cohort aging. For the oldest age groups, upper-age categories (particularly ages 85 and older) are consolidated to adjust for the fact that all individuals aged 85 and older are reported as one category, so the 75 to 84 cohort and the 85 and older cohort combine over a ten-year period.

ii. Limitation of Census Data in Rural Communities

The United States Census American Community Survey (ACS) provides critical, granular data related to housing and population for rural communities. At the same time, this data is imperfect. Year-by-year data is based on sampling over a 5-year period, and margins of error are high in rural communities. Reproducing or finding this level of data would be nearly impossible without the American Community Survey, so its limitations are worth it for the data it can provide.

PorchLight works to collect additional data in the communities where we work, including through community interviews, to ensure this data aligns with the reality on the ground.
